# Future of AI: Superintelligence or Normal Technology?

AI is often presented as a revolutionary technology that will either lead to superintelligence or catastrophic
outcomes. Headlines talk about mass unemployment, machines surpassing humans, and companies investing billions in the
expectation that AI will transform entire industries. But there is another, less dramatic way
to view AI: as a normal technology.

In their article AI as Normal Technology, Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor argue that AI should be understood in the
context of previous technologies. This does not mean that AI is unimportant or that it cannot become extremely powerful.
Rather, it means that technological progress does not automatically translate into immediate social transformation.
Innovation can happen quickly, while adoption, regulation, infrastructure, and changes in society can take decades.

AI is exciting, but discussions about its future are often shaped by hype, economic incentives, and dramatic
predictions. Looking at AI as a normal technology allows us to ask more practical questions: How does AI actually become
part of society? What resources does it require? Who benefits, who bears the risks, and who takes responsibility when
things go wrong?

## Definition of AI, AGI and Normal Technology

To understand the arguments presented better, it is useful to define the central terms.

**Artificial Intelligence (AI)** describes systems that perform tasks we associate with human intelligence, such as
learning, reasoning, pattern recognition, and problem-solving.

**Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)** is a broader and less clearly defined concept. It generally refers to an AI
system capable of performing a wide range of tasks across different domains with a high degree of adaptability and
reasoning. What exactly counts as AGI, however, is likely to change over time depending on which capabilities society
considers economically and socially valuable.

The idea of AI as **normal technology** does not claim that AI will never become extremely powerful. Instead, it asks us
to focus on how technologies actually become part of society.Electricity, computers, and the internet did not transform
society overnight. Their effects depended on infrastructure, organisations, regulations, skills, and people changing the
way they worked.

## Innovation Hype vs. Diffusion

One of the most important arguments in Narayanan and Kapoor's article is the distinction between innovation and
diffusion. AI methods can improve extremely quickly, while their actual adoption throughout society can remain
relatively slow. Particularly in high-consequence areas, more capable models do not automatically lead to rapid
deployment.

I find this distinction convincing because technological progress is often confused with technological adoption. A new
AI model can be released within months, but integrating it into a company's workflows can take years. Organisations need
to adapt processes, train employees, restructure responsibilities, address legal requirements, protect sensitive data,
and determine where AI can actually create value.

> Improvements in models do not automatically lead to rapid changes in society.

At the same time, I do not think that slow diffusion means AI will have little economic impact. Research already shows
that AI can produce measurable benefits in specific settings. For example, Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond found that
access to a generative-AI assistant increased productivity among customer-support workers, with particularly large gains
among less experienced workers. Their results also showed substantial differences between groups of workers. This
suggests that AI can create real value without automatically transforming every profession at the same time.[^1]

This is also where the current AI hype becomes interesting. AI companies have strong economic incentives to present
their technology as revolutionary. Investors expect growth, companies compete for market share, and enormous amounts of
money are being invested in computing infrastructure and AI development.

If these expectations are correct, the potential returns are enormous. But the opposite is also possible. If adoption is
slower than expected, or if AI applications fail to deliver the promised value, companies that have invested heavily may
face significant financial pressure. The important question is therefore not whether investing in AI is rational or
irrational, but whether today's expectations about future AI demand will match the economic value that can actually be
realised.

The hype can move faster than the technology's actual integration into society.

### Benchmarks and Real-World Applications

This gap between hype and reality can also be seen in the distinction between AI methods and AI applications.

An AI model is the underlying method that processes inputs and produces outputs. An AI application is the practical
implementation of that method in a specific real-world context. These are not the same thing.

AI models are relatively easy to test through benchmarks. Real-world applications are much more complicated because they
involve users, organisations, legal requirements, unexpected inputs, and consequences that cannot always be anticipated
in advance.

This makes benchmark performance difficult to interpret. An AI system may perform exceptionally well on mathematics or
physics exam questions while still struggling with a real-world task where the correct response depends on incomplete
information, social context, values, or experience.

If AI intelligence is primarily defined by performance on standardised tests, we may end up measuring what is easy to
evaluate rather than what is actually important. The same problem applies to AGI. The definition of "general
intelligence" may shift depending on which capabilities society considers valuable and which tasks AI can perform.

For me, this leads to a simple principle:
> AI should be judged by its application and consequences, not simply by how intelligent the model appears.

## Capability-Reliability Gap

I find Narayanan and Kapoor's distinction between capability and reliability particularly convincing. A model can
demonstrate impressive capabilities in a controlled benchmark while remaining unreliable when placed in an unpredictable
social environment.

Highly consequential real-world situations cannot simply be recreated in a laboratory. A model may perform extremely
well under controlled conditions and still fail when it encounters unexpected circumstances. Testing every possible
deployment condition is practically impossible.

The obvious solution would be to collect more real-world data. Unfortunately, this creates another bottleneck. Important
data is often undocumented, unstructured, private, inaccessible, or simply not shared.In some situations, an AI system
would need to interact with people or experiment in the real world to learn what works. Such experimentation takes time,
requires consent, and can itself create risks.

There is also a deeper problem: sometimes there is no clear rule to learn. Reasonable people can disagree about what is
right or wrong in a particular situation. Context matters. An action that looks harmless in isolation can become harmful
when considered as part of a larger process.

> Some forms of knowledge and decision-making cannot simply be extracted from datasets.

This is why I am not convinced that every limitation of AI can be solved simply by making models larger or faster. In
many situations, the difficult part is not producing an answer quickly but understanding what the situation actually
requires.

## Resources and Responsibility

AI development depends on far more than algorithms. It requires researchers, realistic data, computing power,
electricity, data centers, infrastructure, software, legal frameworks, and organisations willing to change their
workflows.

The environmental dimension makes this especially important. By 2030, AI’s water use will match the needs of 1.3 billion
people while its power use triples that of 650 million.[^2]

This matters environmentally as well as economically. AI is not an abstract intelligence floating in the cloud. It
relies on physical infrastructure, energy, hardware, and human labor.

The question is therefore not simply: "How powerful can we make AI?" but also: "What resources are we willing to spend,
and for what purpose?"

More research does not automatically produce fundamentally new ideas, and more computing power does not necessarily
solve deeper problems of context, reliability, or responsibility.

Responsibility is equally important. If a company deploys an AI system that causes harm, the costs should not simply
disappear into society. Companies deploying AI should be responsible for testing, monitoring, failures, and remediation.
This becomes particularly important for high-risk applications, where mistakes can have serious consequences.

At the same time, regulation creates a difficult trade-off. If an AI system has to demonstrate its capabilities and
safety before being allowed to operate in the real world, how can it gain the real-world experience necessary to
demonstrate those capabilities in the first place? There is no simple solution, but the problem shows that AI
development is not purely a race between algorithms. It is also a question of institutions, resources, responsibility,
and social acceptance.

## Three Theses

Based on Narayanan and Kapoor's arguments, I would summarise my position in three theses.

Thesis 1: AI innovation will progress faster than AI diffusion.
AI models can improve rapidly, but integrating their capabilities into society requires organisational change,
infrastructure, regulation, and trust. The gap between impressive demonstrations and reliable applications will
therefore remain important.

Thesis 2: The main limits of AI are increasingly social, contextual, and material.
More computing power and larger models cannot automatically solve problems involving privacy, missing data, human
disagreement, energy consumption, infrastructure, or responsibility. AI development therefore has physical and social
limits as well as technical ones.

Thesis 3: AI should be evaluated by its real-world consequences rather than by hype and benchmarks alone.
A model being able to solve difficult exam questions does not necessarily mean that deploying it creates meaningful
value. We should also ask who benefits, who bears the risks, how many resources are required, and who is responsible
when the system fails.

## AI in five years

In five years, I expect AI to be much more integrated into existing software and workflows rather than existing mainly
as a separate category of technology. AI assistants will likely be increasingly common in programming environments,
office software, research tools, search, customer service, and administration.

However, I do not expect every impressive AI capability to automatically become a successful real-world application.
Some systems will create substantial value, while others may prove too expensive, unreliable, resource-intensive, or
difficult to integrate. The most consequential applications are likely to face additional requirements related to
safety, regulation, liability, and reliability.

I also do not think the most important question in five years will be whether AI has achieved some specific definition
of AGI. A more useful question will be where AI has actually become reliable and valuable enough to change how people
and organisations work.

## Conclusion

I broadly agree with Narayanan and Kapoor that AI is likely to develop more like previous technologies than the current
hype often suggests. But "normal technology" should not be interpreted as "unimportant" or "harmless." It means that AI
remains embedded in the same kinds of constraints that shape other powerful technologies: infrastructure, resources,
organisations, institutions, economics, regulation, and human behaviour.

AI may become far more capable over the coming years. But capability alone does not guarantee adoption, economic value,
or social transformation. A benchmark score can increase while reliability in real-world situations remains uncertain.
Investment can grow while the resources required to sustain that investment become increasingly significant.

For me, this is what makes the idea of AI as normal technology so compelling. It moves the discussion away from asking
whether machines will suddenly become superintelligent and toward questions that are much more concrete: What is AI
actually useful for? Who benefits from it? Who pays for it? What resources does it consume? What happens when
it fails? And who takes responsibility?

Maybe the future of AI is not about machines suddenly becoming something completely different from technology as we know
it. Maybe it is about something much more ordinary.
