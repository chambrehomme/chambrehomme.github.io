Technology is changing the way we live. We use digital tools to communicate, learn, work, and make decisions. Artificial
intelligence has expanded these possibilities even further. It can help us write, translate languages, analyse
information, and solve problems that once took much longer.

But being able to do something does not always mean we should do it.

As these tools become part of everyday life, we need to think about the choices behind them. Can we trust the
information they provide? What happens to our personal data? And what might we lose if we let technology take over tasks
that require human judgment, care, or responsibility?

![Beyond AI: Intro](/assets/img/beyond-ai-intro.jpeg)

On October 19, 2026, five Christian church organisations in Switzerland published a joint statement titled *Humanity in
the Digital Age: Ethical Guidelines for the Responsible Use of Artificial Intelligence: An Ecumenical Christian
Perspective*. [^1]

The statement sets out ten principles for approaching artificial intelligence with care. Its starting point is that
technology should serve human beings, not the other way around. Although the guidelines come from a Christian
perspective, many of the questions they raise matter to society as a whole.

Here is what these ten principles mean in practice.

## 1. Human Dignity Matters More Than Efficiency

Computers can process information at remarkable speed. They can recognise patterns, automate routine tasks, and help
people work more efficiently. But efficiency is not the same as human worth.

The statement places human dignity at the center of its approach to artificial intelligence. People should not be valued
only by their productivity, skills, or usefulness. Our lives involve relationships, experiences, responsibilities, and
needs that cannot be fully measured by a computer.

This matters when automated systems are used to assess people, rank applications, or make decisions about access to
services. A score may summarize certain information, but it cannot tell the whole story of a person's life.

Technology can also make a real difference for the better. Tools that help people with disabilities communicate or
access information can give them greater independence. The important question is whether a system supports people or
reduces them to data points.

> Technology should respect people's dignity, even when treating everyone as a number would be faster or
> easier.

## 2. Digital Communication Cannot Replace Every Human Connection

Messages, video calls, translation tools, and speech recognition help us communicate across distances and barriers.
These are valuable uses of technology, especially when meeting in person is difficult.

Yet the statement also draws attention to the importance of physical presence and direct human relationships. Being with
someone involves more than exchanging words. It means sharing experiences, noticing how another person feels, and
responding to them at the moment.

An AI assistant may help someone organise their thoughts before a difficult conversation. It can suggest words, explain
a problem, or offer a way to get started. But it is not a friend or family member who shares a life with us and takes
responsibility for the relationship.

The risk is not that we use digital tools. It is that we begin to treat digital interaction as a complete substitute for
human contact.

> Use technology to bring people closer, not to make human relationships less important.

## 3. Learning Means More Than Getting the Right Answer

AI tools can explain unfamiliar subjects, translate difficult texts, and help people find information. Used
thoughtfully, they can make learning more accessible.

But there is a difference between receiving an answer and understanding why it is correct.

Learning often involves uncertainty. We try something, make mistakes, ask questions, and gradually work out how things
fit together. That process helps us develop judgment and skills of our own.

If a student asks a tool to complete every assignment, the work may get done without much learning taking place. The
same problem can arise at work when people accept generated reports or recommendations without understanding how they
were produced.

The statement's emphasis on education is a reminder that people need opportunities to develop their own abilities.
Technology can support that process, but it should not remove the need to think, practice, and learn.

> Use AI to help you understand a subject, not simply to avoid the work of understanding it.

## 4. Keep a Sense of Hope Without Ignoring the Risks

New technologies often inspire strong reactions. Some people expect AI to solve almost every problem. Others worry that
it will bring more harm than good.

The statement offers a perspective shaped by hope rather than either blind optimism or fear. Hope does not require us to
believe that every new development will turn out well. It gives us a reason to work toward a better future while being
honest about the problems we face.

AI may help people carry out tasks, access information, and explore new solutions. It can also be used carelessly,
produce misleading results, or contribute to unfair treatment.

These outcomes are not all inevitable. The decisions made by developers, businesses, governments, and the people using
these systems matter.

Instead of asking whether AI is entirely good or bad, we can ask which uses are worthwhile, which risks need to be
addressed, and what safeguards should be in place.

> Welcome useful possibilities, but do not confuse hope with the belief that technology will
> automatically make things better.

## 5. Do Not Mistake a Convincing Answer for the Truth

AI can produce fluent explanations that sound confident and complete. That does not guarantee that the information is
correct.

A generated answer may contain factual errors, leave out important context, or present an uncertain claim as if it were
settled. Search results and other digital information can also reflect the assumptions and biases present in the
material used to produce them.

The statement highlights the importance of knowledge and transparency. People need to understand what a system can do,
where its limitations lie, and when its output needs closer examination.

This is especially important when information is used in reports, academic work, professional decisions, or public
discussions. Check the original sources. Verify quotations and figures. Compare accounts when a subject is disputed. For
decisions with serious consequences, seek appropriate human expertise rather than relying on an automated answer alone.

Being able to explain how a result was reached also matters. When a system influences an important decision, people
should not be left with an answer they cannot question or understand.

> Treat AI-generated information as something to examine, not something to accept simply because it
> sounds convincing.

## 6. Freedom Means Being Able to Question Decisions

Digital systems shape much of what we see and do. Recommendation algorithms select videos and articles, platforms decide
which content receives attention, and automated tools can influence decisions about work, education, and public
services.

These systems may be useful, but they also raise questions about freedom and participation. Who decides how they work?
Can people understand the choices being made on their behalf? Can they challenge a decision that affects them?

The statement calls attention to the importance of human freedom and participation. People should not lose their ability
to make meaningful choices simply because a technical system has become involved.

For example, if an automated system rejects an application or recommends a significant decision, there should be a
meaningful way to review the result where appropriate. A person should not have to accept an outcome merely because it
came from a computer.

People also need a voice in decisions about how these technologies are introduced into their workplaces, schools, and
communities.

> Technology should support informed choices and meaningful participation, not make important decisions
> impossible to question.

## 7. Privacy Is About More Than Keeping Secrets

Our digital lives leave behind a great deal of information. Online services may collect details about our interests,
habits, location, purchases, and conversations. AI systems can make use of such information in ways that are not always
obvious to the people providing it.

The statement emphasises integrity and privacy. These principles ask us to consider not only what information is
collected, but also how it is used and whether people's trust is being respected.

Personal information can help provide useful services. But collecting more than necessary, sharing data without proper
safeguards, or using information in unexpected ways can put people at risk.

This deserves particular care when information concerns health, finances, private conversations, or other sensitive
parts of life. Before entering personal details into an AI service, consider whether the service is suitable for that
information and what its privacy terms say.

Organisations also have a responsibility to handle people's information carefully. Privacy should not depend entirely on
whether an individual remembers to check every setting.

> Personal information belongs to people's lives, not simply to the systems that collect it.

## 8. Digital Progress Has a Physical Cost

Digital technology can feel weightless. A file appears on a screen, an answer arrives in seconds, and information moves
around the world almost instantly. Behind these experiences, however, are physical devices, data centers, electricity,
water, and raw materials.

The statement includes responsibility for creation and the environment among its guiding principles. From this
perspective, technological progress must be considered alongside its effects on the natural world.

Producing electronic devices requires resources. Running digital infrastructure consumes energy, and discarded equipment
contributes to electronic waste. AI services also depend on computing infrastructure whose environmental costs should
not be ignored.

Technology can contribute to environmental research and help improve how resources are used. But these possible benefits
do not remove the need to consider its own footprint.

Individuals can help by keeping devices for longer, repairing them when possible, and avoiding unnecessary upgrades.
Businesses and developers can consider energy use, equipment lifetimes, and the resources required to run their
services.

> A technology's value should be considered alongside the resources it consumes and the consequences it
> leaves behind.

## 9. The Benefits of Technology Should Be Shared Fairly

AI does not develop in a vacuum. Building and operating advanced systems requires computing power, data, money, and
skilled workers. As a result, a relatively small number of organisations can gain considerable influence over the tools
that other people and businesses depend on.

The statement's principle of justice raises a wider question: who benefits from technological change, and who carries
its costs?

Access is one part of the issue. Some people have reliable internet connections, modern devices, and the skills needed
to use new tools. Others face financial, educational, or physical barriers. If these differences are ignored, new
technology can deepen existing inequalities.

Power also matters. When essential services depend on systems controlled by a small number of providers, people may have
little say over the conditions under which those services operate.

Fairness requires more than making new tools available. It also means considering working conditions, accountability,
access, and the interests of communities affected by technological development.

Governments, businesses, and public institutions all have a role in ensuring that the advantages of digital technology
do not remain concentrated in a few hands.

> Ask not only what a technology can achieve, but also who gains from it, who bears the costs, and who
> gets a say.

## 10. We All Have a Part in Shaping the Future

Technology changes the way we work, communicate, learn, and organise society. Those changes are too important to leave
entirely to the companies that build digital systems or the experts who understand their technical details.

The statement calls for people to take collective responsibility for the transformation brought about by AI. That means
involving society in decisions about how these systems are developed and used.

Workers should be able to discuss changes that affect their jobs. Students and teachers should have a voice in how AI is
used in education. Communities should be able to raise concerns when new systems affect their access to services or
their daily lives.

Participation also means accepting our own responsibilities. Developers need to consider the consequences of what they
build. Organisations need to think carefully before deploying automated systems. Users need to check information and
make considered choices about how they rely on these tools.

We cannot predict every consequence of new technology. But we can decide to approach it with care, listen to people who
may be affected, and change course when something is not working.

> The future of AI is not just a technical matter. It is a shared responsibility.

## Putting the Ten Principles Into Practice

The statement covers dignity, relationships, education, hope, knowledge, freedom, privacy, the environment, justice, and
collective responsibility. Together, these principles offer a practical way to think about technology before deciding
how to use it.

Here are a few questions worth asking:

- **Does it respect people?** Consider whether a tool supports human needs or reduces people to scores and categories.
- **Am I still learning?** Use AI to explore ideas and improve your skills rather than handing over every difficult
  task.
- **Can I trust this information?** Check important claims and look for sources that support them.
- **Do people have a choice?** Consider whether a system allows decisions to be questioned and reviewed.
- **Is personal information protected?** Think carefully before sharing private or sensitive data.
- **What is the environmental cost?** Consider energy use, equipment, and whether a new tool is actually needed.
- **Who benefits?** Look at who gains access, who makes the decisions, and who might be left behind.
- **Who is responsible?** Remember that using a computer system does not remove human responsibility for its
  consequences.

These questions apply whether you are using an AI assistant to write a report, choosing software for a business,
introducing digital tools into a classroom, or deciding how much information to share online.

## Final Thoughts

The ten principles are not a checklist. They are a way to think about technology in relation to people and society. They
are a reminder that the choices we make about technology can have lasting effects on our lives, our communities, and the
world we share. Technological progress is not just about making systems faster or more capable. It is also about
deciding what place those systems should have in our lives.

A tool can save time and still create problems. A system can be efficient and still treat people unfairly. An answer can
sound authoritative and still be wrong.

That is why the ten principles matter. They bring the discussion back to things that are easy to overlook when a new
technology captures our attention: human dignity, real relationships, independent thought, freedom, privacy, fairness,
and care for the environment.

![Beyond AI](/assets/img/beyond-ai-keeping-humanity-at-the-center.jpeg)

The statement expresses these ideas through a Christian understanding of humanity and responsibility. Its message also
offers questions that reach beyond religious boundaries. Whatever our beliefs, we have reasons to care about how
technology affects people and the world we share.

We do not need to reject every new tool, nor do we need to accept every new development. We can choose where technology
is useful, question where it creates risks, and take responsibility for the decisions we make.

Ultimately, the goal is not to fit human life around technology. It is to make sure technology has a place in human life
that is worth having.
