# Future of AI: Superintelligence or Normal Technology?

AI is often presented as a revolutionary technology that will either lead to superintelligence or catastrophic
outcomes. Headlines talk about mass unemployment, machines surpassing humans, and companies investing billions in the
expectation that AI will transform entire industries. But there is another, less dramatic way
to view AI: as a normal technology.

In their article AI as Normal Technology, Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor argue that AI should be understood in the
context of previous technologies. This does not mean that AI is unimportant or that it cannot become extremely powerful.
Rather, it means that technological progress does not automatically translate into immediate social transformation.

## Innovation vs. Adoption

> AI innovation will progress faster than AI diffusion.

One of the most important arguments by Narayanan and Kapoor is the distinction between technological innovation and
diffusion. AI models can improve rapidly, while their adoption in society can take much longer. A new model can be
released within months, but integrating it into an organisation may require years of new infrastructure, training,
regulation, workflow changes, and trust. Particularly in high-consequence areas, more capable models do not automatically lead to rapid
deployment.

This is not unique to AI. Previous technologies such as electricity and computers also required major organisational
changes before their full effects could be realised. AI is likely to face similar limitations.

At the same time, slow adoption does not mean that AI will have little economic value. Research by Brynjolfsson, Li, and
Raymond, for example, found that generative AI increased productivity among customer-support workers. This shows that AI
can create real value without transforming every profession simultaneously.[^1]

The current AI hype therefore creates an interesting tension. Companies are investing heavily because they expect
enormous future returns. But if adoption is slower than expected, or if AI applications fail to provide the promised
value, these investments may become financially difficult to justify. **The hype can move faster than the technology's
actual integration into society.**

## Capabilities measured by Benchmarks Are Not Applications

> AI should be evaluated by its real-world consequences rather than by hype and benchmarks alone

Another important distinction is between AI models and AI applications. A model can perform extremely well on a
benchmark without being reliable enough for a real-world task.

For example, an AI system may solve difficult mathematics or physics problems while struggling with a professional
situation where the correct decision depends on incomplete information, social context, or human values. Benchmarks
measure what is easy to test, but real-world applications are much more complicated.

This creates what I see as a **capability–reliability gap**. A system can demonstrate that it is capable of performing a
task without demonstrating that it can perform that task reliably in all the situations that matter.

More data does not necessarily solve this problem. Important real-world information may be private, undocumented,
unstructured, or simply unavailable. In addition, some decisions have no universally correct answer. Context and human
judgment can matter more than the amount of information available.

For this reason, I do not think that every limitation of AI can be solved simply by making models larger or faster. The
difficult part is often not producing an answer, but understanding **what the situation actually requires**.

> Some forms of knowledge and decision-making cannot simply be extracted from datasets.

## Resources and Responsibility

> The main limits of AI are social, contextual, and material, not simply computational.

AI is also not an abstract intelligence floating in the cloud. It depends on researchers, data, computing power,
electricity, data centres, hardware, infrastructure, and human labour. This makes AI development an environmental and
economic issue as well as a technological one.

The question is therefore not only:**How powerful can we make AI?** It is also: **What resources are we willing to
spend, and for what purpose?**

More computing power cannot automatically solve problems involving reliability, context, or responsibility. Some
limitations of AI may therefore be social or institutional rather than purely technical.

Responsibility is especially important for high-risk applications. If a company deploys an AI system that causes harm,
the costs should not simply be transferred to society. Companies should be responsible for testing, monitoring,
failures, and remediation. This also raises a difficult question: if AI must prove that it is safe before being used in
the real world, how can it gain the real-world experience necessary to demonstrate that safety?

## AI in Five Years

In five years, I expect AI to be much more integrated into existing software and workflows. AI assistants will likely
become increasingly common in programming, research, customer service, and automation.

However, I do not expect every impressive AI capability to become a successful real-world application. Some systems will
create substantial value, while others may prove too expensive, unreliable, resource-intensive, or difficult to
integrate. High-consequence applications will probably face stronger requirements concerning safety, regulation, and
liability.

I also do not think the most important question will be whether AI has achieved a specific definition of AGI. A more
useful question is where AI has become reliable and valuable enough to actually change how people and organisations
work.

## Conclusion

I broadly agree with Narayanan and Kapoor that AI is likely to develop more like previous technologies than the current
hype suggests. However, this does not mean that AI will be insignificant. Its capabilities may progress rapidly, while
adoption, infrastructure, regulation, and social acceptance develop much more slowly.

For me, this is what makes the idea of AI as normal technology so compelling. Instead of focusing only on whether
machines will become superintelligent, we should ask more practical questions:

**What is AI actually useful for? Who benefits from it? What resources does it consume? What happens when it fails? And
who takes responsibility?**

The future of AI may therefore be less about a sudden transition to something completely different and more about
figuring out how to make a powerful technology actually work in the real world.
